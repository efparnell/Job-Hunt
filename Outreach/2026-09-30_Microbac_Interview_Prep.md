## 1. The strategic frame — read this first

**This is the only role in the pipeline where you are not a career-changer.** Everywhere else you argue that operating skill transfers. Here you are a lab person who became an operator. That is not a compromise position, it's the scarcest profile in commercial testing.

The market has two kinds of candidates for this seat:
- **The scientist promoted upward.** Knows the methods, has never built a profit plan, avoids hard conversations about people, treats the P&L as something finance does.
- **The operator parachuted in.** Can read a variance report, can't tell whether a technical director's explanation for a missed turnaround is legitimate or a story.

You're the third thing. **Say some version of this out loud in the interview**, because Long will not infer it: *"I came up through analytical chemistry and lab operations, and then spent my career learning to run businesses. I can sit with a technical director and know whether the answer I'm getting is real, and I can sit with your CFO and own the number."*

**What the job actually is.** A regional P&L across several accredited labs, reporting to a COO at a family-owned company that has acquired at least three labs in about eighteen months. Read between the lines: the region probably has inconsistent performance site to site, the acquisitions are probably not yet on common methods, metrics or systems, and the COO cannot personally be in five places. They are hiring a set of hands and a standard, not a scientist.

**Comp is no longer the issue.** Your last base was about $129k, this range goes to $130k, and the Stellar work continues. Play for scope and trajectory, not for squeezing the top of the band. More on the money conversation in section 6.

## 2. Your five core stories, pre-loaded

Have these ready as stories, not claims. Each needs a situation, what you did, and a number.

**A. The turnaround: sales +30%, costs −40% in four months.**
This is your headline. Prepare the *mechanism*, because any competent interviewer will ask how. Be ready on: what you measured that nobody was measuring, what you stopped doing, what you changed about scheduling or staffing, and what you did *not* cut (capability, accreditation, quality). For a lab audience, the last part matters most — they are afraid of an operator who cuts QC to make a month.

**B. Multi-site scale: 8 locations, 300+ employees, $10M operating budget.**
The point is not size, it's *variance*. Eight sites that each believed they were unique, and what you did to make them comparable: common definitions, a shared reporting cadence, site-level accountability. Land the line: "The hardest part of multi-site work isn't the distance, it's getting eight sites to agree on what a number means."

**C. Quality systems as an operating system: ISO 9001 and ISO 17025.**
Your 17025 experience is a credential in this room and nowhere else in your pipeline. Prepare a story where the quality system *made* the operation better — a corrective action that fixed a process, an audit that exposed a real workflow defect — not one where you merely passed an audit. Most operators treat accreditation as tax. Treat it as instrumentation.

**D. ERP and systems implementation.**
The lab analogue is LIMS. Be honest that you've implemented ERP rather than LIMS, then show you understand why it's the same fight: adoption, data discipline, sequencing, and what happens when people keep the old spreadsheet running in parallel. Ask which LIMS each of their labs runs — if the acquired labs are on different ones, you've found the region's biggest hidden cost.

**E. Green Analytics — your most recent lab-side credibility.**
This is your proof you're current, so don't let it go unmentioned. Before tomorrow, write out three things: what the operation was, what you owned, and one measurable thing you changed. *(Fill this in yourself — I don't have the specifics and you should not improvise them on the call.)*

**F. Current Stellar work.**
You are not unemployed, you're operating right now. Frame it as active consulting with invoiced project work through this month, and be ready with one sentence on the kind of problem you're solving. Then pivot to why you want a durable seat with a team and a P&L rather than project work. Do not sound apologetic, and do not sound like you'd keep it as a side business unless you intend to disclose that.

## 3. Questions they will ask, and how to handle each

**"Walk me through your background."**
Ninety seconds, chronological, with a spine: chemistry and lab operations → quality systems and accreditation → multi-site operations and P&L → why that path leads here. End on the reason you want *this* seat, which is that it's the first role you've seen that needs both halves of your career at once.

**"How current are you technically? You haven't been at the bench in years."**
Do not overclaim. The honest, strong answer: *"I'm not going to out-chemist your technical directors, and I shouldn't. What I keep current is enough method fluency to ask the right question and know whether an answer holds up."* Then give the hexavalent chromium example as an operations point — the prep queue, courier timing, instrument uptime and analyst certification behind a short-hold-time analysis are what set turnaround time and margin. One example. Move on.

**"How would you run a P&L across several labs?"**
Answer in a cadence, not a philosophy. Weekly: sample backlog, on-time turnaround, open corrective actions, overtime. Monthly: revenue and margin versus plan by lab, cost per sample, capacity utilization, revenue per analyst and per instrument, reanalysis and rework rate. Quarterly: capital, capacity planning, pricing. Then the important sentence: *"The first thing I'd do is find out whether all the labs calculate those the same way. Usually they don't, and you can't manage a region until they do."*

**"One of your labs is missing turnaround times. What do you do?"**
Diagnose before prescribing, out loud. Is it sample intake and scheduling, prep capacity, instrument uptime, analyst certification coverage, QC failure and reanalysis, or reporting and review at the end? Each has a different fix, and the last one — data review bottlenecked on one or two approvers — is the most commonly misdiagnosed. Say you'd go look at the queue before you'd add headcount.

**"How do you integrate an acquired lab?"**
The best answer respects the asset: *"You bought them for their client relationships and their people, so the first rule is don't break either."* Sequence: keep client-facing continuity intact, standardize the measurement layer first (what gets counted, how, when), reconcile methods and QC practice second, then systems. Be explicit that you'd protect the acquired lab's local relationships during the transition rather than immediately re-badging everything.

**"A client needs data today and QC says rerun."**
There's a right answer and they're testing whether you know it. The data doesn't go out. You call the client yourself before the deadline rather than after it, you explain what happened in terms they can act on, and then you fix the system that created the surprise. Add: *"The reputational asset a lab sells is defensibility. You can recover from a late result. You don't recover from a result that doesn't hold up."* A family-owned company with a name since 1969 will feel this.

**"Half this job is travel. How do you manage sites you're not standing in?"**
Concrete: a standing cadence per site, a small consistent metric set reviewed the same way every week, site leaders who own their numbers, and travel spent on the site that needs it rather than rotating evenly. Say you use travel for developing people and for problems that need to be seen, not for presence theater.

**"How do you hire and keep analysts right now?"**
Acknowledge the labor market honestly. Then talk about the levers that actually retain bench staff: certification and cross-training paths, schedule predictability, career steps that don't require leaving the bench, and reducing the rework that makes good analysts feel like they're on a treadmill. Wage is one lever and rarely the decisive one on its own.

**"Tell me about managing out a site leader who wasn't performing."**
Have one. Show a real sequence: clear expectations, documented, with support offered, then a decision made in a reasonable timeframe rather than dragged for a year. Long came up through the operation; he's watched people avoid this.

**"Your career has covered a lot of industries. Why?"**
Your own framing, which is genuinely strong: it's one specialty, not many. Organizations where growth outran the management systems, and building the systems and leaders to scale. Then name why this one is the consolidation of it rather than another swerve.

**"Why Microbac?"**
Three specifics, not flattery: (1) family-owned and independent in a sector being rolled up by Eurofins, Pace and SGS, which means service and defensibility are the differentiation and operations is how you deliver it; (2) recent acquisitions mean the interesting work is real and immediate; (3) the seat needs a lab person who can own a number, which is your actual profile. Do **not** call them PE-backed.

**"What are your salary expectations?"** — see section 6.

## 4. What you ask them — this is where you win the room

Ops candidates get hired on their questions more than their answers. Ask these in roughly this order and take notes visibly.

**Diagnostic (what am I walking into):**
1. Which labs are in the Northeast region, and how does revenue split across them?
2. Is this a new seat or a backfill? If it's a backfill, what did the last person find hardest?
3. Which lab in the region worries you most right now, and why that one?
4. When you look at the region monthly today, what do you see and what do you wish you saw?

**Integration (the real work):**
5. Where are Mosaic, Geoanalytical and GPL in the integration? Same LIMS? Same method suites? Same pricing logic?
6. What's been hardest about absorbing acquisitions so far — systems, people, or clients?
7. Is more acquisition activity likely in the Northeast in the next year?

**Authority and scope — ask these, they define whether the job is real:**
8. Do the lab or site managers report to me directly, and who owns hiring decisions for them?
9. How is commercial ownership split — do I own revenue in the region, or partner with sales?
10. What's the capital process for instrumentation? Who decides, and how fast?
11. What can I change without asking, and what comes to you first?

**Success:**
12. Twelve months from now, what has to be true for this to have been a good hire?
13. What does the region look like in three years if the growth plan works?

Question 8 is the one that has burned you before. Tesla and Havtech were both scope traps — impressive-sounding roles with no real authority. Get a clear answer.

## 5. The 90-day plan — your differentiator

Bring this. Almost nobody does, and for an operations role it converts the conversation from "can you do this" to "here's how I'd start." One page, offered late in the conversation: *"I put together how I'd approach the first ninety days — can I leave this with you?"*

**Days 1–30: measure and listen. Change almost nothing.**
Visit every lab in the region. Sit with each lab manager, each technical or QA lead, and a few bench analysts. Pull the last twelve months by lab: revenue, margin, cost per sample, on-time turnaround, reanalysis rate, overtime, open corrective actions, instrument downtime. Determine whether every lab defines these the same way — expect that they don't. Identify the top five clients per lab and what they'd say if called.

**Days 31–60: install one common operating cadence.**
One weekly metric set, defined identically at every lab. One monthly operating review per lab with the same agenda: performance to plan, capacity, quality, people. Fix the definitions before the targets. Publish the region's numbers so sites can see each other; internal comparability drives more improvement than any target I could set from Baltimore.

**Days 61–90: one visible win and a plan.**
Pick the single most expensive fixable problem found in the first thirty days — usually a turnaround bottleneck or a reanalysis rate — and fix it end to end at one lab, with a number attached. Then deliver a written regional plan for the next twelve months: capacity, capital, staffing, integration sequencing.

**A line to say out loud:** *"I'd rather be slow for thirty days and right afterward than reorganize something in week two that I don't understand yet."* Operators who've been burned by a fast-moving newcomer will hear that.

## 6. The money conversation

Your position is stronger than you were treating it. Prior base ~$129k, the posted range tops at $130k, and Stellar income continues. So this is not a concession, and you should not negotiate like it is one — but you also shouldn't leave money on the table by being agreeable.

**If they ask for a number:** *"Your posted range works for me. Based on my last base and the scope here, I'd be looking at the upper end of it, and I'd want to understand the bonus or incentive structure that sits on top."* That's honest, anchors at $130k, and opens the total-comp conversation without a fight.

**Do not** volunteer that you'd accept less, don't disclose the Stellar income as a reason you can take a lower base, and don't mention the old $150–200k target — it's not this market's number for this seat and raising it only creates friction.

**Worth asking about:** bonus or profit-share and how it's calculated, whether a regional P&L carries an incentive tied to plan, travel reimbursement and vehicle or mileage policy, health coverage and retirement match (real money at this level), and the review cycle timing. A family-owned company often has better benefits continuity and worse formal comp structure than a sponsor-backed one — find out which.

**Trajectory question worth asking directly:** *"If this region performs, what does the next step look like here?"* At a company buying labs, regional roles often become divisional ones.

## 7. Metrics and vocabulary — speak the operating language fluently

Have these on the tip of your tongue. Using three or four naturally signals you've run a lab P&L.

**Financial and productivity:** revenue and margin to plan by lab · cost per sample · revenue per analyst FTE · revenue per instrument · capacity utilization by instrument and by shift · overtime as a percent of direct labor · billable versus non-billable analyst hours · sample volume mix by matrix and method · price realization versus list.

**Operations:** on-time turnaround percentage (and how it's defined — received-to-reported, or scheduled-to-reported, which is where labs hide) · sample backlog and aging · prep queue depth · instrument uptime and downtime causes · hold-time violations · subcontracted or outsourced test volume · courier and logistics cost per sample.

**Quality:** reanalysis and rework rate · QC failure rate by method · corrective and preventive actions open and aging · proficiency-test performance · audit findings and closure time · data review cycle time (the hidden bottleneck at most labs — results sit waiting for an approver) · chain-of-custody exceptions.

**People:** analyst certification coverage by method (the constraint nobody tracks until someone quits) · cross-training depth · turnover by site and by role · time-to-productivity for a new analyst.

**The systems vocabulary:** LIMS · ISO/IEC 17025:2017 · ISO 9001 · TNI standard and NELAP accreditation · A2LA and PJLA as accrediting bodies · state drinking-water certifications · method validation and verification · MDL and LOQ · control charts and control limits · CAPA · uncertainty of measurement.

## 8. Technical refresher — enough to be conversant, not to pretend

Lightly, and only where it comes up naturally. **Verify anything you plan to state as fact.**

**Hexavalent chromium** — your own way in. EPA **218.7** is the drinking-water method, ion chromatography with post-column derivatization; **7196A** is the colorimetric approach and **7199** the IC method on the waste side. The operations story is the short hold time: sample preservation and scheduling drive courier timing and prep sequencing, and blown hold times are a pure-loss failure mode, since the sample has to be recollected at someone's expense.

**PFAS** — the growth engine in environmental testing right now. **537.1** and **533** for drinking water, **1633** for non-potable water, soils and biosolids. Operationally: expensive instrumentation, brutal contamination control requirements, long run times, and demand driven by federal drinking-water regulation. If the Northeast labs do PFAS, capacity and capital are likely the region's biggest constraint and its biggest growth lever. **Ask about it.**

**Routine environmental work:** metals by **200.8** and **6020** (ICP-MS); volatiles by **524.2** in drinking water and **8260** on the waste side; semivolatiles by **8270**; wastewater conventionals (BOD, TSS, nutrients); microbiology for total coliform and E. coli.

**If they run food or life-sciences work:** pathogen testing (Salmonella, Listeria), nutritional labeling panels, shelf-life studies, and the relevant FDA or USDA registrations. Ask what mix the Northeast region carries — it changes the operating model substantially, since food labs run shorter turnarounds and tighter seasonal peaks.
