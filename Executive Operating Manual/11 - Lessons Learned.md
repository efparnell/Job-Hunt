
# 11 - Lessons Learned
## Executive Operating Manual
### Evan Forrester Parnell

> *"Strategy is not defined by the plans you make. It is refined by the feedback you receive."*

---

# Purpose

This chapter captures the practical lessons learned throughout the executive search. Unlike the preceding chapters, which establish philosophy and methodology, this chapter records observations from the market itself.

These lessons were earned through applications, interviews, recruiter conversations, networking efforts, and careful evaluation of opportunities. They materially changed the search strategy.

---

# Lesson 1 — Titles Are Weak Predictors

Early in the search, emphasis was placed on titles such as COO, VP Operations, and Director of Operations.

Over time it became clear that title alone revealed very little.

A "General Manager" might own an entire P&L and lead hundreds of employees, while a "VP Operations" role might function primarily as a technical specialist.

The business problem mattered more than the title.

**Decision:** Evaluate scope, mandate, and organizational need before considering title.

---

# Lesson 2 — Product Expertise Can Trump Executive Capability

The Carter Machinery process was a pivotal learning experience.

The interview progressed well, but the selected candidate ultimately possessed direct industry and product knowledge.

The lesson was not that transferable leadership lacked value.

The lesson was that some organizations are solving for technical familiarity rather than operational transformation.

**Decision:** Prioritize organizations seeking operators over organizations seeking product experts.

---

# Lesson 3 — Every Rejection Carries Information

Rejections from companies such as Vacation Inc., Amplify, Alyssa Nobriga, Albireo, Cobalt Service Partners, and others were not viewed as failures.

Instead, each rejection became market data.

Questions asked after every outcome included:

- Was this truly a strong fit?
- What business problem were they hiring to solve?
- Did the résumé emphasize the right strengths?
- Was industry expertise likely decisive?

Over time, patterns emerged.

---

# Lesson 4 — Narrow the Search to Improve Results

The instinct during a slow search is often to apply more broadly.

The opposite proved more effective.

The search narrowed toward:

- Industrial services
- Technical services
- Environmental services
- Equipment-intensive businesses
- Founder-led organizations
- Private equity portfolio companies
- Multi-site operating businesses

This increased strategic alignment.

---

# Lesson 5 — The Executive's Profession Is Organizational Engineering

Perhaps the most significant realization of the search was conceptual.

The Executive is not defined by laboratories, analytics, startups, or ecommerce.

The recurring profession is designing organizations that scale.

This insight reshaped résumés, interviews, networking conversations, and target-company selection.

---

# Lesson 6 — Protect Time Ruthlessly

Not every opportunity deserved an application.

Several postings appeared attractive because of title or compensation but ultimately required:

- niche certifications
- deep product expertise
- defense experience
- extensive construction project delivery
- highly specialized logistics backgrounds

Applying to every possible role reduced focus.

Passing quickly became a competitive advantage.

---

# Lesson 7 — Small Signals Matter

Language inside job descriptions became an effective screening tool.

Positive indicators included:

- professionalize operations
- scale
- KPI development
- build systems
- improve execution
- develop leaders
- integrate acquisitions

Negative indicators included:

- product specialist
- decades of industry experience required
- highly technical credentials
- singular functional ownership

Words often revealed the true business need.

---

# Lesson 8 — Networking Multiplies Strong Fit

Networking proved most valuable when attached to an already excellent opportunity.

Examples included reaching out to hiring managers or executives after determining the business problem aligned closely with the Executive's strengths.

Networking could improve visibility.

It could not compensate for weak strategic fit.

---

# Lesson 9 — Private Equity Represents a Long-Term Opportunity

As the search evolved, private equity emerged as one of the strongest long-term career paths.

PE-backed organizations repeatedly exhibited the exact challenges the Executive had spent his career solving:

- scaling
- integration
- operating cadence
- KPI discipline
- leadership development
- founder transition

Rather than becoming merely another target, private equity became a strategic focus.

---

# Lesson 10 — Every Conversation Refines the Story

One of the most valuable outcomes of the search was not an interview.

It was clarity.

Repeated conversations gradually refined the Executive's narrative until it became increasingly concise:

The Executive helps technically capable organizations become operationally excellent organizations.

That single statement proved more powerful than any list of titles.

---

# Search Principles Going Forward

The following principles should govern future searches:

1. Start with the business problem.
2. Evaluate organizational maturity.
3. Apply selectively.
4. Tailor documents deliberately.
5. Capture lessons after every outcome.
6. Continue refining positioning.

---

# What Success Looks Like

Success is not measured solely by accepting an offer.

Success is measured by finding an organization whose needs align closely with the Executive's unique capabilities.

Such alignment increases:

- impact
- satisfaction
- long-term growth
- organizational value creation

---

# Closing Reflection

The executive search itself became an operational improvement project.

Hypotheses were formed.

Experiments were conducted.

Feedback was analyzed.

The strategy evolved.

That process mirrors the Executive's broader philosophy: continuous improvement through disciplined learning.

The greatest product of the search was not merely a stronger résumé.

It was a clearer understanding of the Executive's professional identity and the kinds of organizations where that identity creates the greatest value.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Treat market feedback as operational data.
2. Narrowing the search improved opportunity quality.
3. Organizational fit matters more than title.
4. Product expertise is not always transferable—and that is acceptable.
5. The search process itself should continuously improve.
