## What this conversation is for

Chris has already decided she wants him. Kelly has already cleared the consistency and comp questions. This is the owner deciding whether to approve a six-figure hire into a family business.

So he is not re-testing laboratory competence. He is asking three things, mostly without saying them out loud:

1. **Is this someone I want in the company for a long time?** Family companies measure tenure in decades. Chris has twenty years. This is the dominant question.
2. **Will he represent us well?** With clients, with staff at sites he inherits, and inside a culture that calls itself a family.
3. **Is the money sound?** Not whether $120,000 is affordable, but whether this person returns more than that in a region that has been running without an owner.

---

## The four things to land

**1. Longevity, stated plainly.** This is the one that needs saying out loud, because the resume raises it: Atlantic Technical Services five years, Atlantic Test Labs two, Atlas Labs two, Green Analytics four. None of those were failures, but a family-company president is counting.

The honest frame: the moves were a trajectory, not restlessness. Each one was a step up in scope, and two of them were businesses he built that reached their natural end. **What he wants now is the thing he has not had: a seat with real authority inside a company big enough to keep growing into.** Say that.

**2. Why independence matters to him.** Grayson belongs to ACIL. His family has kept this company out of the roll-up for fifty-seven years while Pace, Eurofins and SGS bought everything around them. Evan's line from the outreach email works here and should be said to his face: *independence only holds if the operations hold.* That is a reason to hire an operator, framed as a reason to admire the company.

**3. What the region actually returns.** This is the money answer, and it should be concrete rather than enthusiastic. Baltimore fixed, Richmond behind it, the rest of the territory characterized within a year. Turnaround and retention are the levers, and both carry revenue: a lab that misses dates loses accounts, and a lab that loses analysts loses certified method coverage, which is capacity.

**4. That he knows what he does not know.** The plan's thirty days of characterization before changing anything is the single best evidence of judgment in the whole packet. Owners worry about a new hire breaking something that works. Say the restraint out loud.

## The longevity answer, settled 10/20

The one question in this conversation with a wrong answer. Say it before he has to ask, if there is a natural opening.

> *"Let me say the thing I assume you're thinking when you look at my resume. There's a five, a two, a two and a four on there. Two of those were companies I helped build that reached their natural end. Atlantic Test Labs got acquired. Atlas was my own, and the market we built it for didn't materialize. Green Analytics capped out and needed a chemist in that seat more than it needed an operator, and I'm the one who told them so. None of it was restlessness, but I understand how the pattern reads from the outside.*
>
> *What I'd tell you is that I'm done with that shape of a career. At this stage what I want is one place for the next ten or fifteen years where I'm not thinking about the next move. Some of that is where I am in life. Most of it is that the combination I've ended up with, the laboratory side and the operations side, only really pays off if you stay somewhere long enough to build with it. Four years isn't long enough to see the second-order effects of anything you put in.*
>
> *Microbac is the first place I've looked at where that's realistic. You've been independent since 1969, people here count tenure in decades, and the network is still growing, so there's room to grow into without having to leave to get it. I'd rather be the person who fixes Baltimore, then takes the region, then takes on more than that, than someone who does eighteen good months and moves on."*

**Why it is built this way.** Naming the pattern first takes the weapon off the table, and an owner respects a candidate who says the uncomfortable thing unprompted. The explanations are specific and checkable, and two of them are not about him leaving at all, which is what a resume cannot show. "I'm the one who told them so" is the credibility detail. The want is then stated plainly with a business reason attached, so it reads as professional interest rather than a wish for a safe harbor. The close is about this company specifically.

**The load-bearing line is the second-order effects one.** It reframes long tenure as the condition under which his kind of work pays off at all. That is what separates this from wanting somewhere quiet to land.

**Three things to avoid:** sounding tired of work rather than tired of churn, letting "security" imply coasting, and any hint of defensiveness about the resume.

## Questions he is likely to ask, with answers

Drafted 10/20 from Evan's own material. Adjust wording to voice; the structure is what matters.

### "Why Microbac, and why this role?"

> *"Three things. First, you're independent in a sector that's being rolled up. Pace, Eurofins, SGS and ALS have bought most of what used to be the regional independents, and you've been at this since 1969 without selling. That matters to me because independence only holds if the operations are strong enough to defend it, and that's the work I do. Second, the role itself needs someone who can sit with a technical director and know whether the answer holds up, and also own a number. That's an unusual combination, and it's the one I actually have rather than one I'd have to argue for. Third, and I'll be honest about it, Baltimore is a straight run up from where I live. I want to be in the labs, not describing them from a distance."*

### "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

> *"Running this region well enough that it isn't the problem it is today, and then carrying more of the network. I'd like the Mid-Atlantic to be where the operating standard got proven, and then be useful putting it anywhere else you're growing. I'm not angling for anybody's chair. I want scope that grows inside one company, which is the thing I haven't had."*

Do not say "your job." Do not say "wherever the company needs me."

### "What would make you leave?"

> *"The same thing that ended the last one. Being somewhere my skills stopped being needed. That's what happened at Green Analytics when the market capped and what they needed next was technical depth. A company that's still acquiring and still building out a national network doesn't really have that failure mode, as long as I keep being useful as it grows. The other version would be a seat where I couldn't make decisions, and that's a question I'd rather settle now than discover in month four."*

### "What's your management style?" (the culture-fit question in a family company)

> *"Two rules I've operated by my whole career: do no harm, and respect expertise. A lab that's been running for years is running for reasons, and my first job is to understand them. After that it's fairly simple. Clear standards, published, so people know what's expected and how to move up. Numbers visible, so nobody gets surprised. And managers who own their results instead of passing them up to me. The thing I'm proudest of from Green Analytics isn't the throughput. It's that by the end, the middle managers handled a serious personnel situation correctly, by the book, before I'd even heard about it."*

It ends on the managers acting without him on purpose. In a family company the question behind the question is whether he builds people or only processes.

### "What do you need from us to succeed?"

> *"Four things. Clarity on which sites are mine and how the P&L is cut. Defined decision rights, so I know what I can approve and what goes to Chris first. An introduction to whoever I'd work with on the finance side. And thirty days of questions before I start proposing changes."*

### "What concerns you about the role?"

> *"One thing, and I'd rather raise it than discover it later. A regional director who can't make decisions about purchasing, capital or staffing turns into a reporting function. I've been in a version of that before, and it's the one thing I wouldn't want to repeat. Nothing in the conversations so far suggests that's what this is, but it's the question I'd want answered plainly."*

The riskiest answer in the set and the most valuable. It is the only place the authority question can be asked without sounding like a demand, and it pairs with the inputs list rather than competing with it.

### "Tell me about yourself"

Possible even at this stage. Use the ninety-second version from the Kelly prep: chemist, built labs, ran a big one, why this role. End on the present.

### Compensation

If he raises it, hold at $120,000 and do not reopen it. If he offers more, thank him and move on. Do not negotiate against an owner in an approval meeting. Anything to settle goes back through Kelly afterward.

## What to ask him

These should be owner-level questions, not operations questions. Chris already answered those.

1. **What does Microbac look like in five years?** Bigger network, same independence, or something else?
2. **How do you think about the balance between acquiring labs and growing the ones you have?** He described an acquisition model built on buying from retiring sole proprietors; this invites the strategy behind it.
3. **What makes someone successful here for a long time?** Direct, and it tells him tenure is on Evan's mind in a good way.
4. **What would you want to be true about the Mid-Atlantic a year from now?** The owner's version of the success-criteria question, and his answer may differ from Chris's.
5. **Is there anything about my background that gives you pause?** The same closer that worked with Kelly. Owners especially appreciate being asked directly.

## Tone notes

- **He is not a technical interviewer.** Avoid method numbers, accreditation clause references and PFAS detail unless he raises them. Translate everything into business terms: capacity, retention, client confidence, cost.
- **The 90-day plan is the artifact.** Chris has it. Assume Grayson has seen it or will. Be ready to talk through the exit criteria in plain language: *this is how we will both know Baltimore is fixed, rather than me telling you it is.*
- **Family company, so warmth is not optional.** Competence has already been established twice. This conversation is substantially about whether people will want to work with him for years.
- **Do not oversell.** An owner approving a hire is listening for the overstatement that signals a risk. Understatement with numbers behind it is the register.
