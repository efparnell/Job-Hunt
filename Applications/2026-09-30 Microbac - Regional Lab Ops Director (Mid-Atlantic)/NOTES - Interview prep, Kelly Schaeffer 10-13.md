## What this conversation actually is

Not a technical interview and not a negotiation. She is deciding three things:

1. **Is he consistent?** Do the dates, titles and numbers hold together across the resume, the ADP profile, LinkedIn and his own answers.
2. **Does the money work?** $120,000 was on the application. She is confirming that is still true and that he won't walk at offer time.
3. **Will he fit, and will he stay?** She is an HR director at a family-owned company with twenty-year tenures. Retention is her professional problem.

**Keep PFAS, the 2027 deadlines and the hub-and-spoke analysis out of this call.** Those belong to Chris. Raising them here signals not knowing who you're talking to.

## The strongest card with this person

She is an HR director. He is the operations leader who took annual turnover **from twelve departures to four** — by building a competency framework, **in partnership with Regional HR**. That is her function's hardest metric, solved by an operator who treated HR as a partner rather than an obstacle. Say the partnership part explicitly.

That is compensation structure, career pathing and training governance in one artifact, built by an operations leader. It is, nearly literally, her job.

## The answers

> *"I'm a chemist by training — B.S. from the Naval Academy — then the Navy and a couple of years in the merchant marine, which is where I learned to run a watch and a crew.*

Spine: chemist → built labs → ran a big one → why this. Over ninety seconds, cut the Navy, not the numbers. **Never end on cannabis.**

### "Are you working right now?"

> *"I stepped out of the Associate Laboratory Director seat in October of last year and moved to a consulting relationship with them. If she asks why the consulting isn't on the resume: *"I kept that resume focused on the laboratory record, since that's what this role is. The consulting is commercial systems work rather than lab operations."* Only if asked.

### "Why did you leave Green Analytics?"

> *"The work I was brought in to do was finished. We'd built the management infrastructure the lab needed, and then the market flattened out — what the business needed next was technical depth more than another operator. I told them that, and that they'd be better served putting a chemist in that seat. Since then I've consulted for them and for Stellar Scientific. No C-suite, no ceiling, no mention of the sale. *"I told them to put a chemist in that seat"* does all the work the complaints would have done, at none of the cost.

### "When could you start?"

Two to three weeks. Not "immediately," which invites questions about current commitments.

### "Your resume shows turnover dropping. What did you actually do?"

> *"When I got there we were losing about twelve people a year. I spent roughly six months watching before I proposed anything — I wanted to be sure the problem was the management structure and not the people. What I found was that the management practices hadn't scaled: they were built for the original twelve-person lab, and we were now thirty-five people across two shifts, seven days a week, inside a regional network.*
>
> *Working with Regional HR and the C-suite, we built a leveling framework — Technician and Analyst levels one through three, published pay ranges, documented skill requirements, supervisor sign-off — so there was a visible path and a defined standard. The culture shifted over the first year. Within two years every one of the original fifteen had either advanced into a level or didn't meet the standard the framework made explicit and moved on. By the third year we were at about one departure a quarter, all voluntary — people leaving for school or better jobs.*
>
> *The reason it mattered beyond morale: in a lab, capacity is certified method coverage, not headcount. Every analyst who walks takes qualified methods with them. Going from twelve departures a year to four is what let us take turnaround from five days to three."*

### "Tell me about a time you had to let someone go."

> *"The first one I walked our lab manager through was a technician with a chronic absence problem — frequently out, little notice, often none. In a lab that's not an inconvenience, it's a coverage problem: we'd stopped building her into the shift plan at all, which meant we were short certified coverage on methods she was qualified to run.*
>
> *We worked with HR to put together a performance improvement plan — specific goals, dated checkpoints — and I coached the lab manager through delivering it rather than doing it for him. It held for a couple of weeks, then the old pattern came back. At thirty days we sat down and went through the evaluation together. We terminated her employment. It was emotional, and I told her we'd point her toward whatever help she needed, but that I couldn't run a shift schedule around attendance we couldn't rely on. It was about as straight and as decent as that conversation can be.*
>
> *The sequel is the part I'm proudest of. About a year after the leveling program went in, a technician made statements that disturbed the staff. By the time the lab director and I heard about it, the middle managers had already notified HR and Regional Ops, exactly per protocol. There were health considerations involved, so HR owned it from there — which was the right place for it. It took a couple of months to work through, and the outcome was that she didn't return to the lab. That's when I knew the structure had taken: they didn't need me to handle it, and the part that wasn't mine to handle went where it belonged."*

Say **terminated**, not "separated." Give the outcome, never the medical detail.

### "A major client needs results today and QA says rerun. They're threatening to walk."

> *"The data doesn't go out. If we can't defend the result in court, we don't publish it — that's not a judgment call, and a lab that makes it one doesn't have anything to sell.*
>
> *What is a judgment call is everything around it, and it's all about speed. Within a couple of hours I'd have the technical lead for that area, QA and the site's lab director in one conversation: confirm the rerun is genuinely the recommendation, get the realistic time, and find out what drove it — reagent lot, instrument drift, technique, sampling — because a rerun is a signal, not just a delay.*
>
> *Then the client gets a call, before their deadline, not after it. Whoever owns that relationship makes it, with me on it if it's escalated this far. What happened, when they'll have defensible data, what we're doing so it doesn't recur, and anything we can give them in the meantime.*
>
> *The asymmetry is what makes the decision easy: a late result is recoverable. A result that doesn't hold up isn't — not for them, and not for us."*

### "How would you handle coming in above long-tenured technical leaders?"

> *"Two rules I've operated by my whole career: do no harm, and respect expertise. A lab that's been running eleven years under the same technical director is running for reasons, and my first job is to understand them before I change anything.*
>
> *I've done this exact pairing before. My lab director at Green Analytics was a PhD organic chemist — he owned the science and everything outside the building, and I owned everything inside it: people, workflow, throughput, the facility. Four years, and it worked. He's one of my references, and honestly he's the right person to ask how it held up.*
>
> *So practically: the first thirty days are site visits, time at the bench, and asking people what gets in their way. I'm not going to out-chemist a technical director and I shouldn't. What I'd own is whether the work moves, whether the numbers mean the same thing at every site, and whether we're making money doing it. Technical authority stays where the license does.*
>
> *And when we disagree — science and data defensibility, their call, full stop. Schedule, capacity, cost, how we define a metric, that's mine. In my experience most friction isn't really a disagreement, it's two people optimizing for different things nobody wrote down."*

### "Why Microbac?"

> *"Two reasons. You're one of the few independent, family-owned networks left in a sector that's consolidating hard — and that independence only holds if the operations hold. And the role is written for someone who's both a laboratory person and an operator, which is what I actually am rather than something I'd have to argue for. Baltimore being a straight run up from home doesn't hurt."*

### "What's a weakness?"

Make it real and bounded, with the correction attached. The honest one: *"I'd rather take thirty days to characterize a problem than move in week one, and that patience can read as slow to people who want immediate action. What I've learned is to say out loud what I'm doing and when the recommendations are coming, so it's visibly deliberate rather than quiet."*

### "Are you interviewing elsewhere?"

Honest and brief. Don't invent pressure; don't claim an empty pipeline either. *"I'm being selective — this is the role I've put real effort into."*
