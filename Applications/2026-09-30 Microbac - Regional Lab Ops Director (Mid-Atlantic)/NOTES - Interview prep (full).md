**"One of your labs is missing turnaround times. What do you do?"**
Diagnose before prescribing, out loud. Is it sample intake and scheduling, prep capacity, instrument uptime, analyst certification coverage, QC failure and reanalysis, or reporting and review at the end? Each has a different fix, and the last one — data review bottlenecked on one or two approvers — is the most commonly misdiagnosed. Say you'd go look at the queue before you'd add headcount.

You don't recover from a result that doesn't hold up."* A family-owned company with a name since 1969 will feel this.

## 5. The 90-day plan — your differentiator

Bring this. Almost nobody does, and for an operations role it converts the conversation from "can you do this" to "here's how I'd start." One page, offered late in the conversation: *"I put together how I'd approach the first ninety days — can I leave this with you?"*

**Days 1–30: measure and listen. Change almost nothing.**
Visit every lab in the region. Sit with each lab manager, each technical or QA lead, and a few bench analysts. Pull the last twelve months by lab: revenue, margin, cost per sample, on-time turnaround, reanalysis rate, overtime, open corrective actions, instrument downtime. Determine whether every lab defines these the same way — expect that they don't. Identify the top five clients per lab and what they'd say if called.

**Days 31–60: install one common operating cadence.**
One weekly metric set, defined identically at every lab. One monthly operating review per lab with the same agenda: performance to plan, capacity, quality, people. Fix the definitions before the targets. Publish the region's numbers so sites can see each other; internal comparability drives more improvement than any target I could set from Baltimore.

**Days 61–90: one visible win and a plan.**
Pick the single most expensive fixable problem found in the first thirty days — usually a turnaround bottleneck or a reanalysis rate — and fix it end to end at one lab, with a number attached. Then deliver a written regional plan for the next twelve months: capacity, capital, staffing, integration sequencing.

**A line to say out loud:** *"I'd rather be slow for thirty days and right afterward than reorganize something in week two that I don't understand yet."* Operators who've been burned by a fast-moving newcomer will hear that.

## 8. Technical refresher — enough to be conversant, not to pretend

Lightly, and only where it comes up naturally. **Verify anything you plan to state as fact.**

**Hexavalent chromium** — your own way in. EPA **218.7** is the drinking-water method, ion chromatography with post-column derivatization; **7196A** is the colorimetric approach and **7199** the IC method on the waste side. The operations story is the short hold time: sample preservation and scheduling drive courier timing and prep sequencing, and blown hold times are a pure-loss failure mode, since the sample has to be recollected at someone's expense.

**PFAS** — the growth engine in environmental testing right now. **537.1** and **533** for drinking water, **1633** for non-potable water, soils and biosolids. Operationally: expensive instrumentation, brutal contamination control requirements, long run times, and demand driven by federal drinking-water regulation. If the Northeast labs do PFAS, capacity and capital are likely the region's biggest constraint and its biggest growth lever. **Ask about it.**

**Routine environmental work:** metals by **200.8** and **6020** (ICP-MS); volatiles by **524.2** in drinking water and **8260** on the waste side; semivolatiles by **8270**; wastewater conventionals (BOD, TSS, nutrients); microbiology for total coliform and E. coli.

**If they run food or life-sciences work:** pathogen testing (Salmonella, Listeria), nutritional labeling panels, shelf-life studies, and the relevant FDA or USDA registrations. Ask what mix the Northeast region carries — it changes the operating model substantially, since food labs run shorter turnarounds and tighter seasonal peaks.
